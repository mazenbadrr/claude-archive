Introduction

The IEGR 512 class held a seminar on artificial intelligence (AI) in project management, moderated by Dr. Stephen Egarievwe over Zoom. Each student had about two minutes to present a view on any aspect of the topic, including applications, results, advantages and disadvantages, regulation, and economics, as long as it connected to project management. Students presented in alphabetical order by last name, and the class then held an open discussion on public trust, data privacy, and government policy.

The presentations covered a wide range of settings: construction scheduling and risk, supply chains, traffic management, finance, electronic health records, data-center HVAC upgrades, and owner reporting. Jeremiah Akinlade and Alisa Alford focused on predictive risk analysis; Izuchukwu Igwe, Beakal Nigussie, and Laura Amaya described AI note-takers that free people to engage in meetings; and Taja Wilder explained how she uses AI to draft weekly and monthly owner reports from the superintendent’s three-week schedule. One theme ran through almost all of them. AI moves project management from reacting to problems toward predicting them, but its output is only as good as the data it receives and must stay under human judgment (Egarievwe, 2026). This report summarizes my main contribution, the contributions of two classmates, and the conclusions of the seminar.

My Main Contribution: AI Across the Planning and Control Cycle

My contribution argued that AI is most useful when it is built into the core tools of project control rather than used as a separate chatbot. I organized it around four functions: scheduling, risk and cost prediction, monitoring and control, and resource allocation.

Automated CPM scheduling. The critical path method (CPM) identifies the longest chain of dependent activities, and any delay to a critical activity, which has zero total slack, delays the whole project (Badiru, 2021). In practice, schedules are often updated weekly or monthly, so a slip can go unnoticed until the next update. I proposed that AI can build the initial CPM network from the activity list and dependencies, then recompute it continuously as progress data arrive. When a task slips, the AI can immediately flag whether it has used up its slack and whether the critical path has shifted to a new chain of activities. For example, if a non-critical activity with 2 days of total slack slips by 3 days, it becomes critical and the project finish moves out by 1 day. A weekly manual update might reveal this a week later, while an AI-linked schedule would show the new critical path the same day, when recovery options such as adding resources or overlapping activities are still affordable. This turns the schedule from a static report into a live control tool.

Risk and cost prediction. AI can learn from the records of past projects, such as planned versus actual durations, change orders, and cost reports, and estimate the likelihood of delays and cost overruns on the current project. Instead of relying only on single-point estimates or a fixed contingency, the project manager receives an early warning based on what actually happened on similar work. These predictions then feed into the estimates for the next project, so the organization's estimating improves over time. Adeola Taiwo gave a matching example from data-center cooling upgrades, where AI compares the job with past projects to warn early about long-lead equipment and failed leak tests (Egarievwe, 2026).

Monitoring, control, and communication. On a project with many members, much of the manager's time goes into distributing updated schedules, reminding people of upcoming tasks, and tracking which document version is current. AI can automate this work by sending each member a daily email of their tasks and deadlines, circulating the latest schedule and file versions, and alerting the manager when an expected update is missing. This directly supports Badiru's (2021) Triple C model of communication, cooperation, and coordination, because timely and consistent information is the basis for cooperation and coordination across the team.

Resource allocation and leveling. Finally, AI can spot overloaded team members by comparing assigned work against each person's capacity across the schedule. It can then suggest how to level the work, for example by shifting non-critical activities within their available slack or reassigning tasks, so that peaks in demand are smoothed without extending the project duration (Badiru, 2021). Because leveling uses slack first, it links directly back to the CPM analysis: the AI knows which activities can move without delaying the finish date and which cannot.

Across these four functions, AI checks results and predicts the next steps, but the project manager still makes the decision. The value lies in faster detection and better-informed choices, not in replacing the manager's judgment.

Contributions from Other Students

Syed Musanif Hussain Kazmi: AI from the WBS to the schedule in construction. Kazmi, a civil engineer, linked AI to the earliest stage of planning. Given a project's scope, specifications, drawings, and deliverables, AI can help break the project into a work breakdown structure (WBS) of smaller deliverables and work packages, and it can flag scope that appears to be missing. Because the WBS is the base for scheduling, resource planning, cost estimation, and risk analysis, an error caught at this stage prevents errors in every later plan. He named tools already in use in construction: nPlan, which analyzes schedules to predict where delays are most likely; ALICE, which compares alternative construction schedules and resource scenarios; and Procore's AI features, which work with requests for information (RFIs), submittals, drawings, and daily reports. He also cited an industry article reporting that organizations using AI-enhanced project management delivered about 61% of projects on time, compared with 47% for organizations not using AI, and that 64% of their projects met or exceeded the expected return on investment, compared with 52% for non-adopters (Egarievwe, 2026). His contribution complements mine directly: an AI-generated WBS supplies the activity list from which the CPM network I described is built.

Kenna Kassa: AI for team planning and its limits. Drawing on her experience as a product manager working with a software team at Dell Technologies, Kassa focused on the people side of a project. AI can improve task allocation and workload balance by matching assignments to team members' strengths and progress reports. Tools such as Jira now include AI features that flag a team member who may not finish a task on time, so the manager can offer help before a deadline is missed. The same data can inform sprint planning and retrospectives, and historical spending data can show where project costs can be reduced. She was equally clear about the limits. AI can only use the input it is given, and much relevant information about team members is never entered, so its recommendations can be unfair. It can also weaken the person-to-person relationship that is central to managing a team, which is why a project manager must still know the team and be able to decide without AI. In the open discussion, she extended this concern to electronic health records: patients fear their medical information will be used without permission, and providers worry that the data entered will be inaccurate. The remedies suggested in the literature she reviewed are education on AI use and multiple checkpoints and security measures in how AI tools are integrated (Egarievwe, 2026). Her workload-balance point parallels my resource-leveling point, while her cautions add the human limits that a purely technical view can miss.

Conclusion

The seminar showed broad agreement on what AI does well in project management. It predicts delays and cost overruns from historical data, keeps schedules and team communication current, and removes routine administrative work such as meeting notes and status reports, so that managers can focus on leadership. Classmates from construction, finance, healthcare, and HVAC repeated the same caution: AI is only as reliable as its input data, its output must be verified, and accountability for decisions stays with people.

The closing discussion identified trust as the main barrier to adoption. Classmates noted that confidential financial, medical, and proprietary engineering data should not be placed in AI tools without safeguards. Dr. Egarievwe asked how government can protect data privacy without slowing national AI development, and the class pointed to education, organizational policies, and continued review by Congress as parts of the answer (Egarievwe, 2026). My own takeaway is that the strongest use of AI is inside established project management methods, such as CPM scheduling, risk management, and resource leveling, where it speeds up detection and analysis while the project manager keeps the final decision.
