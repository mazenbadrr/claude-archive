Slide 8 / 23 · forces

The four forces

Four forces act on every rocket

Thrust: up

The push from the engine, along the rocket.

Weight: down

Mass × gravity, pulling at the balance point (CG). Shrinks as fuel burns.

Drag: against the motion

Air pushing back. Twice as fast means four times the drag.

Lift: sideways, on the fins

Only when the rocket tilts. It pushes the tail back so the nose points straight.

Rocket Lab · Day 1 · 08

Before showing this, draw a rocket on the whiteboard and hand her the marker: "Draw every force you think acts on it." Then compare. Analogy: tug-of-war (engine up, gravity down), walking through a swimming pool (drag), feathers on an arrow (fins). Watch for two mistakes: thinking lift holds the rocket up (thrust does) and thinking drag always points down (it points opposite the motion; on the way down it points up). Drag formula for you: D = 1/2 x air density x speed squared x Cd x area.

Slide 9 / 23 · liftoff

Liftoff and flight

Liftoff happens when thrust beats weight

Model rockets aim for thrust at least 5 times their weight, so they leave the launch rod fast enough for the fins to keep them straight.

1

Powered

Motor burns; thrust beats weight + drag, so it speeds up.

2

Coast

Motor is out but it keeps rising, slowing down.

3

Apogee

The highest point. Upward speed is zero for an instant.

4

Recovery

A small charge pops the parachute; it floats down.

Rocket Lab · Day 1 · 09

Ask: "If thrust only needs to beat weight, why use so much more?" Answer: to accelerate fast, fight drag, and be moving fast off the rod so the fins work. Ask: "Does weight change during flight?" Yes, the motor burns propellant, so the rocket gets lighter and accelerates harder. The coast phase is Newton's first law in action.

Slide 10 / 23 · newton3

Newton's third law

Throw gas backward, and the rocket goes forward

The skateboard test

Stand on a skateboard and throw a heavy ball forward. You roll backward. You pushed the ball; the ball pushed you.

A rocket does the same

It throws tiny bits of gas out the back, extremely fast. Faster gas, or more of it, means more push.

Does a rocket need air to push against?

No. It pushes on its own exhaust. Rockets even work a little better in space.

Rocket Lab · Day 1 · 10

This answers the opening question. Ask the dark-box question BEFORE clicking to reveal it. Follow-ups: "If you throw the ball faster, do you roll faster?" (yes: exhaust velocity) "If you throw two balls?" (yes: mass flow). For you: thrust = mass flow rate x exhaust velocity + (exit pressure - ambient pressure) x exit area. With zero ambient pressure in space, thrust goes up slightly.

Slide 11 / 23 · three-laws

Newton's laws

All three laws, on a rocket

LawIn plain wordsOn a rocket
1st: inertiaThings keep doing what they're doing unless a force changes it.It sits on the pad until thrust beats weight. After the motor stops, it keeps coasting up.
2nd: F = m × aMore force, or less mass, means more acceleration.As fuel burns, the rocket gets lighter and speeds up faster.
3rd: action and reactionEvery push comes with an equal push back.The motor pushes gas back; the gas pushes the rocket forward. That's thrust.

Rocket Lab · Day 1 · 11

Keep the 2nd law light today; next week (Oct 21) goes deeper with RocketModeler. Acceleration = (thrust - weight - drag) / mass. A newton is about the weight of an apple; a small Estes C6 model motor averages about 4.7 N of thrust.

Slide 13 / 23 · balloon-build

Part 2 · Activity · 10:45

Build a balloon rocket

You need

  • Long balloons
  • About 5 m of smooth string
  • A drinking straw
  • Tape and a binder clip
  • A tape measure

1

Thread the string through the straw. Tie it between two chairs, tight and level.

2

Blow up the balloon and clip the neck. Tape it under the straw, neck pointing back.

3

Release the clip. Measure how far the straw travels.

Rocket Lab · Day 1 · 13

Check for latex allergy first. Let her build it; you just hold a chair. Say: "The balloon is our engine. The stretchy rubber squeezes the air out, like the helium in our real rocket. The neck is the nozzle. And there's nothing behind it to push against: it pushes on its own air." If it spins, that's thrust not lined up with the center: real rockets need thrust through the CG too.

Part 3 · Our real rocket · 12:30

Our lab's liquid rocket test stand

High-pressure helium pushes two liquids into the engine, where they burn. No pumps: the gas does the pushing.

Helium

High-pressure gas that squeezes the tanks

Two tanks

LOX: liquid oxygen at −183 °C
RP-1: very clean kerosene

Valves and sensors

Remote-controlled valves; pressure and temperature sensors

Engine

Mixes, burns, and blasts the gas out the nozzle

Same propellants as SpaceX's Falcon 9: liquid oxygen and RP-1.

Slide 20 / 23 · why-ground

Static fire

Why we test on the ground first

Measure everything

Bolted to a stand, the engine can't move, so we can record its thrust, pressures, and temperatures.

Fix problems early

Find a leak or a bad valve on the ground, not in the sky.

Stay safe

Valves are controlled from far away, so nobody stands near the engine.

Your final experiment can be the same idea, tiny and safe: a mini test stand that measures a model rocket motor's push.
