Goal: explain what makes one engine push harder than another, using one simple formula.

The NASA page has a full equation. For today, the simple version is enough:

\text{Thrust} \approx \dot{m} \times v_e

That reads: thrust (N) is roughly the mass of gas thrown out each second (kg/s) times how fast it leaves (m/s). The full equation adds a small pressure term at the nozzle exit.

Answer in your notebook

  1. Engine A throws out 2 kg of gas per second at 2,000 m/s. Engine B throws out 4 kg per second at 1,000 m/s. Find the thrust of each.

  2. Both engines have a 100 kg tank of propellant. How many seconds does each engine run? If you were building a rocket to go far, which engine would you pick, and why?

  3. Why does a rocket nozzle get wider toward the end? What does the shape do to the exhaust?

  4. A jet engine stops working in space, but a rocket keeps going. Why?

  5. Our lab's test rig has two tanks: one for liquid oxygen (LOX) and one for RP-1, a kerosene. Which is the fuel, which is the oxidizer, and why must a rocket carry both?

  6. Using the formula, name two ways you could make last week's balloon rocket push harder.

Mission 3: Why rockets drop mass (11:00–12:00)

Goal: use the rocket equation to explain why real rockets are mostly propellant and why they throw away stages.

OrderWatch or readLengthLook for
1Be Smart (PBS): This Could Change the Future of Space Travel11.5 minAstronaut Don Pettit on "the tyranny of the rocket equation"
2NASA: Ideal Rocket Equation10 min readSkip the derivation; find what Δv and mass ratio mean

The rocket equation says how much speed (Δv, "delta-v") a rocket can gain:

\Delta v = v_e \times \ln\left(\frac{m_{full}}{m_{empty}}\right)

v_e is the exhaust speed. m_full ÷ m_empty is the mass ratio: the rocket's mass with propellant divided by its mass after the propellant is gone. ln is the natural log; any calculator or spreadsheet has it.

Mission 1 answers

  1. On the pad, weight pulls down and the pad pushes up by the same amount. Net force is zero, so it stays put (1st law). At ignition, thrust grows past the weight, the forces become unbalanced, and it accelerates up.

  2. Net force = 4.7 − 0.98 = 3.72 N. Acceleration = 3.72 ÷ 0.10 = 37.2 m/s², about 3.8 g (ignoring drag).

  3. Whatever two things push on each other each get an equal push in opposite directions, so both move apart. Accept any correct pair she names from the video.

  4. No. The rocket pushes on its own exhaust gas, and the gas pushes back on the rocket. It works in vacuum, slightly better than in air. This is the most common misconception, so check it.

  5. Sitting still: 1st law (balanced forces). Speeding up: 2nd law (net force gives acceleration; lighter balloon accelerates more). Direction: 3rd law (air pushed back, balloon pushed forward).

Mission 2 answers

  1. A: 2 × 2,000 = 4,000 N. B: 4 × 1,000 = 4,000 N. Same thrust.

  2. A runs 100 ÷ 2 = 50 s; B runs 100 ÷ 4 = 25 s. Pick A: same push for twice as long, so double the total impulse (200,000 vs 100,000 N·s). Faster exhaust means less propellant for the same job. This is the idea behind specific impulse (her Jan 13 topic).

  3. The nozzle narrows to a throat, then widens. Hot, high-pressure gas expands in the bell and speeds up past the speed of sound, so exhaust speed and thrust go up.

  4. A jet engine needs outside air for oxygen. A rocket carries its own oxidizer.

  5. RP-1 is the fuel; LOX is the oxidizer. Burning needs both, and there is no air to supply oxygen in space.

  6. Any two consistent with the formula: more air out per second (bigger opening, bigger balloon) or air leaving faster (higher pressure inside).

Mission 3 answers

  1. The pattern to draw out: every doubling of the mass ratio adds the same 1,733 m/s. Going from 90% to 99% propellant doubles Δv. Each extra bit of speed costs far more propellant than the last.

  2. Only the 99% row. The 9,400 m/s figure is approximate.

  3. The last 1% must hold tanks, engines, structure, and the payload. Real tanks and engines can't be made that light.

  4. Staging resets the mass ratio. Example: two stages that each reach mass ratio 7.4 (about 86% propellant) give 5,000 m/s each, 10,000 m/s total. One stage would need mass ratio 55 (98% propellant) for the same result. This ignores payload, so treat it as a sketch.

  5. Start: 37.2 m/s² (same as Mission 1). End: weight = 0.090 × 9.8 = 0.882 N, net = 3.818 N, a = 3.818 ÷ 0.090 = 42.4 m/s². Same thrust, less mass, more acceleration.

Questions she may ask you

Her questionShort answer
If there's nothing to push against, what does the gas push on?The inside of the chamber. Gas pressure pushes on the closed front wall, but the back is open, so the push is unbalanced and points forward.
Why not just add more engines?Number of engines isn't in the rocket equation. More engines give more thrust (quicker climb, less gravity loss), not more Δv.
What is ln?The natural log. Practical meaning: doubling the mass ratio always adds 0.69 × v_e of speed.
What does delta-v mean?Δ means change. It's how much the rocket can change its speed.
Why do hotter gases give faster exhaust?Hotter, lighter molecules move faster. Save the detail for Jan (nozzles, Isp, CEA).
Why LOX and kerosene?Kerosene is dense and easy to store; LOX is cheap. Falcon 9 and the Saturn V first stage use this pair.
Does a rocket work better in space?Slightly. With no outside air pressure at the nozzle exit, the pressure term in the full thrust equation adds thrust.
